# Endurance training rules
General:
The athlete will have the option whether or not to include endurance sessions in the program.

Make sure to take all other sessions and obligations into consideration when spreading out the endurance sessions for the athlete (this is answered in the standard questionnaire discussed in the top, e.g ..., their sport, number or training sessions in that sport…)

Then, once they select their endurance training form… It will be implemented and designed with consideration of whether they are in camp.off camp… and all other constraints previously asked…

Running is most appropriate when the athlete needs simple, accessible endurance work with strong transfer to general conditioning. Not to mention the benefits of being outdoors in a natural environment. Especially useful in the off-season/off-camp. Its main limitation is impact cost, so it is usually less ideal when the athlete already has a lot of lower-body fatigue, joint irritation, high plyometric exposure, or a heavy combat load.
Circuit Training is most appropriate when the goal is to target a specific fatigue problem rather than just improve generic endurance. It works well when the athlete needs local muscular endurance, repeated-effort capacity, general work capacity, or a blend of qualities in one session. Especially useful for certain sport-specific weak points, such as arm endurance, grip endurance, trunk endurance, or repeated bursts. Its main limitation is its interference with other training days if improperly implemented.
Heavy Bag (For Strikers) is most appropriate when the athlete needs endurance work that stays close to the mechanics and local fatigue of striking. It is useful for aerobic bag work, sustained boxing or kickboxing conditioning, repeated flurry capacity, local upper-body endurance, and sport-specific fight-camp simulation. Its biggest advantage is specificity: it can build conditioning while still reinforcing technical patterns. Excellent choice for the fight camp.
Swimming is most appropriate when the athlete needs low-impact aerobic work with minimal joint stress. It can be useful for general conditioning, recovery-oriented endurance, off-season aerobic development, and athletes who need to reduce impact while still accumulating meaningful aerobic volume. It is also useful when the athlete is beat up from striking, wrestling, running, or lower-body lifting. Its limitation is that technical inefficiency in the water can make it more skill-limited than fitness-limited, so for some athletes it is better as recovery or general conditioning than as a precise conditioning tool.
Assault Bike is most appropriate when the goal is hard conditioning with low impact and simple execution. It is one of the best tools for aerobic intervals, threshold work, hard intervals, repeated-burst training, and mixed upper- and lower-body conditioning. It is easy to scale, provides good metrics, easy to prescribe by time and effort, and hard to “cheat” once the intensity rises. Its main limitation is the rigid and locked movement pattern.
Rowing Ergometer is a simple and accessible tool for total-body steady aerobic work, aerobic intervals, threshold efforts, and hard intervals, especially when the athlete benefits from having a measurable pace output. It also has good value for athletes who want strong trunk and leg involvement without impact. Technique matters; if the athlete rows poorly, the session can become inefficient or overly fatiguing in the lower back and arms.
Skiing Ergometer is most appropriate when the athlete needs upper-body-driven or trunk-integrated conditioning without much lower-body impact. It is especially useful for athletes who want to challenge the lats, trunk, shoulders, and general aerobic system while reducing leg loading. It can work well for intervals, repeated efforts, and mixed aerobic conditioning. It is also a good choice when an athlete already has heavy running, kicking, or leg fatigue in the week. Great for emulating hand-fighting or upper-body work for grappling. Its limitation is that it is less complete as a locomotion tool than running or rowing, so it is often best used as a complementary endurance method rather than the only one.
Arm Crank Machine is most appropriate when the goal is upper-body work or conditioning that avoids lower-body loading almost entirely. It is useful for athletes dealing with lower-body fatigue or injury and for combat athletes whose shoulders and arms fatigue quickly. Excellent for wrestlers. Its limitation is that it is more localized and generally less systemic than modalities involving more total muscle mass, so it is usually best for targeted use rather than as the default endurance method for every athlete.
Sprinting is an exceptionally powerful tool when the goal is maximal-speed exposure, acceleration, repeated high-power efforts, and conditioning that preserves a strong neural and explosive component. Its main limitation is that it carries a relatively high mechanical and hamstring load, so it is usually less suitable when the athlete is highly fatigued, already doing a lot of explosive lower-body work, or does not have the tissue tolerance to sprint safely. It should usually be programmed when speed quality can stay high, not just as a way to make the athlete tired.
