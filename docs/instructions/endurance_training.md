Running is most appropriate when the athlete needs simple, accessible endurance work with strong transfer to general conditioning. Not to mention the benefits of being outdoors in a natural environment. Especially useful in the off-season/off-camp. Its main limitation is impact cost, so it is usually less ideal when the athlete already has a lot of lower-body fatigue, joint irritation, high plyometric exposure, or a heavy combat load.
Circuit Training is most appropriate when the goal is to target a specific fatigue problem rather than just improve generic endurance. It works well when the athlete needs local muscular endurance, repeated-effort capacity, general work capacity, or a blend of qualities in one session. Especially useful for certain sport-specific weak points, such as arm endurance, grip endurance, trunk endurance, or repeated bursts. Its main limitation is its interference with other training days if improperly implemented.
Heavy Bag (For Strikers) is most appropriate when the athlete needs endurance work that stays close to the mechanics and local fatigue of striking. It is useful for aerobic bag work, sustained boxing or kickboxing conditioning, repeated flurry capacity, local upper-body endurance, and sport-specific fight-camp simulation. Its biggest advantage is specificity: it can build conditioning while still reinforcing technical patterns. Excellent choice for the fight camp.
Swimming is most appropriate when the athlete needs low-impact aerobic work with minimal joint stress. It can be useful for general conditioning, recovery-oriented endurance, off-season aerobic development, and athletes who need to reduce impact while still accumulating meaningful aerobic volume. It is also useful when the athlete is beat up from striking, wrestling, running, or lower-body lifting. Its limitation is that technical inefficiency in the water can make it more skill-limited than fitness-limited, so for some athletes it is better as recovery or general conditioning than as a precise conditioning tool.
Assault Bike is most appropriate when the goal is hard conditioning with low impact and simple execution. It is one of the best tools for aerobic intervals, threshold work, hard intervals, repeated-burst training, and mixed upper- and lower-body conditioning. It is easy to scale, provides good metrics, easy to prescribe by time and effort, and hard to “cheat” once the intensity rises. Its main limitation is the rigid and locked movement pattern.
Rowing Ergometer is a simple and accessible tool for total-body steady aerobic work, aerobic intervals, threshold efforts, and hard intervals, especially when the athlete benefits from having a measurable pace output. It also has good value for athletes who want strong trunk and leg involvement without impact. Technique matters; if the athlete rows poorly, the session can become inefficient or overly fatiguing in the lower back and arms.
Skiing Ergometer is most appropriate when the athlete needs upper-body-driven or trunk-integrated conditioning without much lower-body impact. It is especially useful for athletes who want to challenge the lats, trunk, shoulders, and general aerobic system while reducing leg loading. It can work well for intervals, repeated efforts, and mixed aerobic conditioning. It is also a good choice when an athlete already has heavy running, kicking, or leg fatigue in the week. Great for emulating hand-fighting or upper-body work for grappling. Its limitation is that it is less complete as a locomotion tool than running or rowing, so it is often best used as a complementary endurance method rather than the only one.
Arm Crank Machine is most appropriate when the goal is upper-body work or conditioning that avoids lower-body loading almost entirely. It is useful for athletes dealing with lower-body fatigue or injury and for combat athletes whose shoulders and arms fatigue quickly. Excellent for wrestlers. Its limitation is that it is more localized and generally less systemic than modalities involving more total muscle mass, so it is usually best for targeted use rather than as the default endurance method for every athlete.
Sprinting is an exceptionally powerful tool when the goal is maximal-speed exposure, acceleration, repeated high-power efforts, and conditioning that preserves a strong neural and explosive component. Its main limitation is that it carries a relatively high mechanical and hamstring load, so it is usually less suitable when the athlete is highly fatigued, already doing a lot of explosive lower-body work, or does not have the tissue tolerance to sprint safely. It should usually be programmed when speed quality can stay high, not just as a way to make the athlete tired.

## Goal Interpretation Rules for circuit training
The app should not try to train everything equally.
It should preserve full-body training exposure, but give the greatest emphasis to the athlete’s stated weak points. Meaning if someone says “my legs die first” It should NOT be all legs only. 
The algorithm should identify whether the complaint is mainly:
local fatigue, such as shoulders, forearms, trunk, legs
systemic fatigue, where the athlete gasses overall
sport-position-specific fatigue, such as wrestling ties, clinch work, stance maintenance, repeated punching, repeated shots
If the athlete’s goal is vague, the app should prompt them with examples so the complaint becomes more specific.
Example:
“My arms blow up when hand-fighting” = upper-body local endurance
“My legs die first in boxing” = lower-body fatigue resistance + aerobic recovery
“I can go hard once but not repeatedly” = repeated-effort capacity
“I get tired everywhere” = general work capacity, not just one limb
Circuit Construction Rules
The circuit should always include the athlete’s priority area, but should not become so narrow that it ignores the rest of the body.
The primary weakness should receive the most total work, the most demanding slots, or the shortest rest exposure.
Secondary areas should still be included, but with less volume or lower placement priority.
The app should avoid random exercise variety for its own sake. Exercise selection must reflect the actual fatigue problem.
Format Selection Logic
The app should choose the circuit format based on the goal.
If the goal is local muscular endurance
Use moderate work periods and short-to-moderate rest.
Use repeated exposure to the target area without making every station identical.
Keep movement quality stable and avoid excessive technical breakdown.
If the goal is repeated high-effort output
Use shorter, harder bouts with incomplete recovery.
The format should challenge the athlete’s ability to produce effort again before they feel fully recovered.
If the goal is general work capacity
Use broader full-body circuits with moderate station lengths and controlled density.
The goal is sustained output, not all-out collapse.
If the goal is sport-specific fatigue resistance
Use exercises that reflect the actual tissue demands, positions, rhythms, and transitions of the sport.
The circuit should resemble the sport’s energetic pattern without pretending to perfectly replicate the sport itself.
Work:Rest Ratio Rules
The app should not default to one universal Tabata format. It should select work:rest based on the target.
A good logic set would be:
Local endurance emphasis: around 30–60 seconds work with 15–30 seconds rest
General work capacity: around 20–45 seconds work with 15–30 seconds rest
Repeated-effort emphasis: around 10–30 seconds work with equal or slightly shorter rest than needed for full recovery
Very dense “burn” circuits: use sparingly, mainly for small-target local endurance blocks, not as the default format
Tabata-style 20:10 should be an option, not the standard default
Better rule:
The app should choose the least aggressive format that still trains the intended quality.
It should not jump to brutally dense circuits unless the athlete is advanced and the goal truly calls for it.
Exercise Selection Rules
Exercises should match the athlete’s complaint.
If a boxer says the legs fatigue first, the circuit should bias repeated lower-body and stance-demanding work, not just generic burpees.
If a wrestler wants arm endurance, the circuit should bias grip, pulling, pushing, isometric upper-body work, and trunk involvement relevant to wrestling.
The app should distinguish between:
dynamic endurance
isometric endurance
mixed endurance
Sport complaints often involve a mix of both, so the circuit should often include both.
Priority Without Omission Rule
This is important for what you wrote.
The circuit should not leave out other relevant physical qualities entirely.
It should prioritize, not exclude.
A useful rule is:
roughly 50–70% of the circuit stresses the primary target
the remaining 30–50% covers secondary or supportive qualities
That way the athlete gets targeted work without the circuit becoming too one-dimensional.
Session Density Rules
Circuit density should be based on training age, current fatigue, and session role in the week.
If the athlete already has a hard combat week, the app should reduce circuit density before reducing specificity.
Circuits should not become conditioning punishment just because the athlete selected them.
Progression Rules
Progress only one variable at a time:
more total rounds
more work per station
less rest
slightly harder exercise choice
more priority-area exposure
Do not increase all variables at once.
Once technique or position quality drops too much, the circuit is no longer serving the intended purpose.
Placement Rules
Do not place highly fatiguing circuits where they ruin more important sessions.
If the circuit is lower-body heavy, avoid putting it right before key lower-body strength/power or major sparring days.
If the circuit is highly local to a body part already battered by sport practice, reduce volume or shift the day.
Safeguard Rules
The app should avoid selecting exercises that are too technical under fatigue.
When fatigue is the point, exercise complexity should usually go down, not up.
The app should flag when the athlete’s requested goal is better addressed partly through sport practice, strength work, or aerobic work rather than through endless circuit density.
Analytics Rules
After the session, the app should show:
predominant target of the circuit
total work duration
work:rest structure
rounds completed
target-area emphasis
RPE
whether performance dropped sharply across rounds

## Work to rest ratio rules Circuit Training
The work:rest ratio should be chosen by the quality being targeted, not by one default circuit format. Shorter rest increases density and local fatigue; longer rest preserves output quality and repeatability. Broadly, aerobic HIIT often sits around 1:0.5 to 1:1, while repeated-sprint formats use more recovery, often around 1:1 up to 1:6, depending on whether you want more fatigue or better sprint quality.
App-ready rules
Work:Rest Ratio Rules
The app should not default to one universal circuit ratio.
The work:rest ratio should be selected based on the athlete’s primary target.
Shorter rest should be used when the goal is density, local muscular endurance, or general work capacity.
Longer rest should be used when the goal is repeated high-power output, higher movement quality, or better preservation of speed/explosiveness.
As a default rule, the app should choose the least aggressive ratio that still trains the intended quality.
If the athlete is highly fatigued, in-camp, or already carrying a heavy combat load, the app should lengthen rest before reducing specificity.
If technical quality drops too much, the app should increase rest rather than forcing the athlete to survive poor work.

## Stationary methods, ergometers, machines, running…
These can go by:
1) Continuous aerobic
Purpose: aerobic base, recovery, low-cost conditioning.
Best anchors: HR, HRR, pace, power, RPE, talk test.
App rules
Use this mainly for off-season base work, active recovery, or when the athlete needs extra conditioning with low fatigue cost.
Default duration can be 20–45 minutes, not because longer is wrong, but because that is usually enough for combat-sport athletes without eating into recovery. This duration range is a programming recommendation, not a law.
Progress by duration first, then slightly by pace/power.
If the athlete already has a high sport load, prefer bike / row / ski erg / incline walk over extra running as a lower-impact design choice.
2) Aerobic intervals
Purpose: improve aerobic capacity with more control than one long continuous effort.
 Best anchors: pace, speed, power, HR, RPE.
 Typical structure: roughly 2–8 minute work bouts with short easy recoveries. That basic structure is consistent with common aerobic interval models, but the app should individualize it rather than force one template.
App rules
Use for athletes who need aerobic development but do better with broken-up work than long continuous sessions.
Use short recoveries, usually easier than the work but not full recovery.
Default intensity should be controlled hard, not all-out.
Total hard work should usually be built gradually rather than by instantly adding many reps.
3) Threshold / tempo work
This is the category your original text is missing.
Purpose: improve the highest pace/power the athlete can sustain in a relatively steady metabolic state. Critical speed / critical power is commonly treated as a marker of maximal aerobic steady-state or the boundary between heavy and severe domains, which makes it useful for prescribing this kind of work.
App rules
Use this when the athlete has access to running pace, rowing split, cycling watts, or ski-erg pace.
Prefer pace/power-based prescription.
Good structures are either continuous tempo work or longer intervals with short recoveries.
This is often the best “middle ground” when continuous easy work is too easy and HIIT is too costly.
4) High-intensity intervals
This should be split further inside the app, because “HIIT” is too broad.
A. Long HIIT / hard aerobic intervals
 Usually 1–4 minutes hard with 1–3 minutes recovery. These are often used to drive high oxygen uptake and aerobic power. Minimum work bouts of at least about 1 minute for athletes make more sense than trying to call every 10-second burst “VO2 work.”
B. Repeated-sprint training (RST)
 Maximal short efforts of ≤10 seconds with ≤60 seconds incomplete recovery.
C. Sprint interval training (SIT)
 Classically 20–30 second all-out efforts with long recoveries, commonly around 4 minutes, often on a bike/ergometer.
App rules
Use long HIIT for aerobic power and hard conditioning.
Use RST for repeat-burst ability in intermittent sports.
Use SIT sparingly, mainly when the goal is very high metabolic stress and the athlete can recover from it.
Short high-intensity work should be prescribed mainly by time + pace/power/speed + RPE

### Non-Circuit Endurance Rules
The app should classify non-circuit endurance work into continuous aerobic, aerobic intervals, threshold/tempo, or high-intensity intervals, rather than using one generic endurance bucket.
The app should not infer the target system from work:rest ratio alone. It should consider duration, intensity, recovery type, modality, and intended output.
For steady aerobic sessions, the app should use easy-to-moderate intensity, preferably guided by RPE, and talk test.
For aerobic intervals, the app should use longer work bouts and incomplete but controlled recovery, avoiding all-out efforts.
For threshold/tempo work, the app should prioritize pace, speed, or power markers such as critical speed or critical power when available.
For high-intensity interval work, the app should distinguish between long HIIT, repeated-sprint training, and sprint interval training, since these formats have different work durations and recovery demands.
Extra endurance work should usually be kept to the minimum effective dose, especially in combat athletes who already accumulate conditioning from sparring and sport practice.
Hard endurance sessions should be limited and placed carefully around strength, power, and sparring to reduce interference. Concurrent training can modestly blunt some lower-body strength adaptations when poorly organized.

Sport-Specific Heavy Bag Rules
Whether the athlete is a boxer, kickboxer, or thaiboxer matters. E.g., boxer punches only, kickboxer/muay thai kicks.. etc. 
The app should base sport-specific heavy bag sessions on the athlete’s actual or expected bout structure whenever possible.
If the exact bout structure is unknown, the app should use a sport-specific default based on the selected ruleset.
The session should reflect the sport’s round length, rest period, rhythm, and legal technique profile.
The app should offer two modes: exact simulation and overload simulation.
Exact simulation should match bout structure as closely as possible.
Overload simulation should stay close to bout structure, but add difficulty through one main constraint only.
The app should not make sport-specific bag work harder by increasing all variables at once.
The harder the conditioning emphasis, the simpler the combinations and tactical tasks should become.
Technical quality must remain recognizable to the sport. Once balance, guard, posture, or clean mechanics collapse too much, the session is no longer specific enough.
The best overload levers
The app should choose one of these, not several at once:
1. Rest reduction
Keep the same rounds and round length, but shorten rest slightly.
Example: 60 seconds becomes 45 seconds.
2. Round addition
Keep the same round and rest structure, but add one extra round.
Good when you want slightly more volume without distorting pacing too much.
3. Within-round surge constraint
Keep official rounds and rest, but force certain output demands inside the round.
Example: last 20 seconds of every minute = hard flurry.
4. Output quota constraint
Require a minimum tactical or technical action count.
Example: minimum jab volume, minimum kicks per side, minimum punch-kick exits.
Best rule for overload
Only one overload variable should be emphasized per session.
That means:
shorter rest or
one extra round or
built-in surge windows or
output quotas
Not all of them together.

Sport-Specific Fight-Camp Heavy Bag Rules
If the athlete selects sport-specific fight-camp bag work, the app must ask for sport, ruleset, bout format, and whether the session should be exact simulation or overload simulation.
The app should use the athlete’s actual fight format whenever known; otherwise it should default to the selected sport’s standard ruleset.
The session should mirror the sport’s round duration, rest duration, rhythm, and legal technical emphasis as closely as possible.
Overload should be created through one main constraint only, such as slightly shorter rest, one extra round, surge windows within rounds, or output quotas.
The app should not simultaneously increase round count, reduce rest, and raise within-round intensity in the same session by default.
Boxing bag simulations should emphasize punch volume, movement, resets, and controlled flurry demands.
Muay Thai bag simulations should emphasize full-weapon rhythm, kick and knee demands where appropriate, and balance after strikes.
Kickboxing / K1 bag simulations should emphasize punch-kick rhythm, restart speed, and kick volume according to the selected ruleset.
The closer the athlete is to competition, the more the session should prioritize exact simulation over overload.
The session should remain technically recognizable to the sport; once technique degrades excessively, the app should reduce density or volume.
Best guiding sentence
Fight-camp bag work should feel like the sport first, and only then slightly harder through one controlled constraint.

Sprinting can by default only be done as an ANAEROBIC training format–meaning there is not aerobic version. Keep this into consideration for the logic and rules when the athlete is selecting training. 
Sprinting should only appear under anaerobic training options.
The app should not offer sprinting as:
steady-state aerobic work
aerobic intervals
recovery conditioning
If the athlete wants an aerobic endurance session, the app should steer them toward:
running
Assault Bike
rowing ergometer
SkiErg
swimming
arm crank, if lower-body unloading is needed

pending on the athlete and their previous input… The sprinting can be done in the following ways (just suggestions, otherwise stay pragmatic). 
1. Short sprints
Use for: explosiveness, first-step speed, fast entries/exits, shot speed, sharpness
Default rules
Distance: 10–20 m
Reps per set: 4–6
Sets: 2–4
Rest between reps: 60–90 s
Rest between sets: 2–4 min
Simple rule
Keep quality very high
Stop if speed clearly drops
This is speed work, not conditioning
2. Medium sprints
Use for: repeated burst ability, being able to go hard again and again, combat-specific repeat efforts
Default rules
Distance: 20–40 m
Reps per set: 4–8
Sets: 2–4
Rest between reps: 30–60 s
Rest between sets: 2–3 min
Simple rule
Hard efforts, but not sloppy
Best middle ground for many combat athletes
Good for “I can go hard once, but fade after that”
3. Long sprint intervals
Use for: hard conditioning, strong fatigue resistance, running-based HIIT
Default rules
Distance: 60–150 m
Reps per set: 4–8
Sets: 1–3
Rest between reps: 60–180 s
Rest between sets: 3–5 min
Simple rule
This is the most fatiguing option
Use more carefully
Do not place it too close to hard sparring or heavy leg work

Clean app-ready rules
Sprinting Rules
If the athlete selects sprinting, the app should first determine whether the goal is speed, repeat-burst ability, or hard conditioning… Look at their previous inputs and decide. 
Shorter sprint distances should be used for speed and explosiveness.
Medium sprint distances should be used for repeated high-effort ability.
Longer sprint distances should be used for hard conditioning.
The app should reduce total sprint volume when the athlete already has high sparring, wrestling, or lower-body fatigue in the week.
A very clean one-line rule for the app:
The shorter the sprint, the more it is about speed; the longer the sprint, the more it becomes ‘’power’’ conditioning.

The athlete can choose number of endurance sessions (1-7) But, the app will give these guidelines: 
If the athlete has many hard combat sessions, sparring sessions, or long skill sessions per week, stick to the lower end. The higher end is almost never used (app gives volume warning if selected). 
If the athlete has few combat sessions, or not significantly fatiguing ones, add more standalone endurance.
Remind them of the following: 
Start with the lowest number of endurance sessions needed.
Only increase endurance if recovery is stable and performance is not dropping.
Do not add more endurance merely because the athlete selected a high training frequency.
1–2 endurance sessions may be enough for many athletes with a decent sport load
3+ only when the weekly structure truly allows it

There are the structure menu for the endurance training: 
low-intensity aerobic work
aerobic intervals
high-intensity intervals
sport-specific conditioning

Interference rules & combat sports protection rules:
Do not place hard endurance immediately before lower-body strength/power days unless absolutely necessary. 
Do not place high-intensity intervals on the same day as heavy lower-body lifting unless absolutely necessary.
If endurance and lifting must occur on the same day, place the more important quality first (where the athlete is asked). 
If the goal is strength/power, separate hard endurance and heavy lifting by at least several hours when possible.
Low-intensity aerobic work is the safest modality to pair near strength training.
Practical default:
hard endurance far away from squat / explosive lower-body work
easy aerobic work can often sit after upper-body work or on separate recovery days
Combat sports protection:
Rule:
Never stack a hard endurance session the day before an important sparring day if avoidable.
Never place the hardest endurance session on the same day as the hardest sparring session unless the program intentionally uses a high-low loading model.
If the athlete has a key boxing / MMA / Muay Thai session, that session takes priority over generic endurance.
For combat athletes, this matters a lot more than abstract conditioning theory.
