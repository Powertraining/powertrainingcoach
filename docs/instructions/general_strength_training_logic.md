# General Strength Training Logic
## Loading Strategies
### Flat Loading
This is the most basic form of loading for strength training, regardless of the sport, and is recommended for the majority of beginners. You simply take your 1RM on a given lift, and you perform a given percentage of that for multiple sets with the same repetition range. For instance, if you have planned the back squat for 5 sets of 5 reps at 70% of 1RM, then you can start with 3-4 warm-up sets and perform your planned 5x5 at 70% (alternatively, if you use the RPE system, you would determine an RPE for that day and implement warm-up sets until you reach your planned RPE). For the most part, all sets will be of similar difficulty (if you do not accumulate too much fatigue by the end).

### Pyramid loading
Pyramid loading strategies gradually increase or decrease the weight and number of repetitions from one set to the next. There are three main types of pyramid-loading strategies:
 
Ascending pyramid. Start with moderately intense and/or light-volume work, then progress to intense and voluminous work.
Descending pyramid. You start with intense or high-volume work, then progress to less intense and less voluminous work.
Double pyramid. Both pyramids are performed back-to-back. High-to-low and low-to-high.
 
Within the pyramid itself, you can select the following methods:
1. 	Keep the same rep range in every set, but vary the intensity.
2. 	Vary the rep range in every set, but keep the same intensity.
3. 	Vary both the rep range and the intensity in every set.
 
Method 1: Keep the same rep range in every set, but vary the intensity

#### Table 16.4 – Example pyramid—keep the same rep range, but vary the intensity (using percentages)

| Ascending pyramid | Descending Pyramid | Double Pyramid |
|---|---|---|
| Set 1: 5 reps at 70% of 1RM | Set 1: 5 reps at 80% of 1RM | Set 1: 5 reps at 75% of 1RM |
| Set 2: 5 reps at 75% of 1RM | Set 2: 5 reps at 75% of 1RM | Set 2: 5 reps at 80% of 1RM |
| Set 3: 5 reps at 80% of 1RM | Set 3: 5 reps at 70% of 1RM | Set 3: 5 reps at 75% of 1RM |

Method 3: Vary both the rep range and the intensity in every set
If using this format, first we have to decide whether we want to increase the absolute intensity, the relative intensity, or both in each set. If the same relative intensity is applied to all sets, they are expected to be of equal difficulty (but with slight differences in structure). Refer to the previous chapter if you've forgotten what relative intensity is.
 
An example format for an ascending pyramid with equal RI:
·   	Set 1: 5 repetitions at 77% of 1RM (RI: 87.5%)
·   	Set 2: 4 repetitions at 79% of 1RM (RI: 87.5%)
·   	Set 3: 3 repetitions at 81% of 1RM (RI: 87.5%)
 
Alternatively, you may increase the relative intensity for the sets as well:
·   	Set 1: 5 repetitions at 77% of 1RM (RI: 87.5%)
·   	Set 2: 4 repetitions at 81% of 1RM (RI: 90%)
·   	Set 3: 3 repetitions at 86% of 1RM (RI: 92.5%)
 
Obviously, in the second format, each set will feel more difficult due to the increased relative intensity. If using RPE, remember to consider the rep range and its relationship to the RPE point. For example, 3 reps at RPE 8 differ from 5 reps at RPE 8 (since the former has a smaller rep range, you probably will load more weight). Here are some examples:

| Ascending pyramid | Descending Pyramid | Double Pyramid |
|---|---|---|
| Set 1: 5 reps at 75% of 1RM | Set 1: 3 reps at 85% of 1RM | Set 1: 5 reps at 80% of 1RM |
| Set 2: 4 reps at 80% of 1RM | Set 2: 4 reps at 80% of 1RM | Set 2: 4 reps at 85% of 1RM |
| Set 3: 3 reps at 85% of 1RM | Set 3: 5 reps at 75% of 1RM | Set 3: 5 reps at 80% of 1RM |

## How often to change Loading Strategies
A practical rule that works well in software
Keep the session scheme stable for a “block,” and only rotate it at block boundaries or when you hit clear stall signals.
That gives you:
consistent data (so the app can detect trends),


enough exposures for skill/coordination to improve,


novelty when it actually matters.


How long should a “block” be?
Use training age + exercise complexity:
Beginner (0–12 months consistent lifting)
Change scheme rarely: every 8–12 weeks (or even longer).


Default: flat loading (simple, repeatable, measurable).


If they’re bored, change rep range first (e.g., 3×8 → 5×5), not the whole scheme.


Intermediate (1–3 years)
Change scheme every 4–8 weeks at most.


Within that block, you can still undulate intensity/rep targets by day (heavy/medium/light) without changing the scheme.


Advanced (3+ years)
Change scheme every 2–6 weeks depending on proximity to competition and how often they’re exposed to the lift.


Advanced athletes can tolerate (and sometimes benefit from) more frequent within-week variation, but even here: don’t rotate everything constantly—keep at least one anchor (same main lift + same “type” of exposure).


When should the app trigger a change?
Use objective “stall” logic rather than vibes. For a main lift, change the scheme only after at least one of these patterns shows up:
Performance trend stalls


e1RM (or top-set load at given RPE) is flat/down across ~2–3 exposures despite normal recovery.


RPE drift / fatigue creep


Same planned work suddenly costs ~+1 RPE for 2 sessions, or reps degrade earlier than normal.


Compliance drops


Missed sessions, skipped top sets, or consistent under-loading because the scheme is too demanding/time-consuming.


Important: when a stall happens, first try a deload or volume reduction while keeping the scheme. If performance doesn’t rebound after that, then rotate the scheme (or rep range). Don’t change 3 things at once.
Option: The app asks the user how things are going, periodically. And then, it may give recommendations of when or if it’s time to change things up. 
The cleanest approach is:
Objective trend detection (app)


Subjective check-in (user)


One clear recommendation + one-tap options


When should the app ask?
Two layers works best:
1) Weekly mini check-in (10–15 seconds)
Only 2–4 questions


Used to adjust next week’s load/volume, not overhaul the whole scheme


2) End-of-block check-in (every 4–8 weeks depending on training age)
This is where scheme changes live (flat → wave → cluster, etc.)



The actual check-in questions (minimal, high signal)
Keep it extremely quick, mostly multiple-choice:
Progress (choose one)


Improving


Flat


Regressing


Not sure


Effort / fatigue this week


Fresh


Normal


Beat up


Enjoyment / motivation


High


OK


Bored


Pain / irritation (optional but important)


None


Mild (doesn’t affect lifting)


Affects training


That’s enough to drive 95% of good decisions.

Combine user answers with what the app already knows
Your app can compute a simple trend on the main lifts (choose whatever you’re already tracking):
e1RM trend (from top set + reps/RPE), or


best set at a given rep target, or


load at a fixed RPE for a fixed rep target


Also track:
completion rate (missed sessions / skipped top sets)


RPE drift (planned RPE vs reported RPE)


Then let the check-in break ties.

Decision logic that feels “coach-like”
Here’s a practical mapping that avoids dumb changes:
Case A — Progressing + fatigue normal + enjoyment OK
Action: Keep the scheme.
 Optional: small overload next week.
Case B — Progressing/flat + fatigue high
Action: Deload or reduce volume 20–40% for 1 week.
 Do not change scheme yet.
Case C — Flat/regressing + fatigue normal/low
Action: First adjust dose:
add 1 hard set, or


add a small load jump, or


tighten rest times / improve adherence
 If still flat after ~2–3 exposures → consider scheme/rep-range change.


Case D — Flat/regressing + fatigue high
Action: Deload + keep scheme.
 If no rebound after deload → then change scheme or exercise variation.
Case E — Bored but progressing
Action: Offer a “novelty swap” that preserves the stimulus:
same weekly hard sets, same average intensity, new structure (flat → pyramid, etc.)
 Make it explicit this is optional and may slightly reduce predictability of progress.


Case F — Pain affects training
Action: Swap exercise variation and/or reduce intensity/ROM temporarily.
 Scheme change is secondary.

How to phrase it in-app
Have the app show what it detected and ask for confirmation. Example:
“Your squat trend is flat over the last 3 exposures and sessions feel harder than planned.
 Want to keep the plan, deload, or change the structure?”
Buttons:
Keep (recommended)


Deload 1 week


Change loading scheme


Change exercise variation


If they hit “Change loading scheme,” show 2–3 options max, not 8.

One simple “Change scheme” rule (to prevent chaos)
Only allow a scheme change if either:
the block ends, or


two flags are true (e.g., “flat trend” + “bored” / “compliance dropping” / “RPE drift”)


This keeps the program stable and the data clean.

A compact function skeleton (logic)
If you want the app logic in one place:
Inputs:
- trend: up / flat / down (from objective data)
- fatigue: low / normal / high (from check-in + RPE drift)
- enjoyment: high / ok / bored
- pain: none / mild / limiting
- training_age: beginner / intermediate / advanced
- block_week: current week in block

Outputs:
- action: keep | micro-adjust | deload | change_scheme | change_exercise
- explanation: 1 sentence

Default priority:
pain/limitation


fatigue management (deload)


dose tweak (sets/intensity)


scheme change (mostly at block boundaries, or when stuck + bored)

## Reps and sets in accordance to current season/period.
Depending on whether it’s an intense sports period currently, the program needs to be adjusted for that–whether in terms of intensity, reps or sets. 
Thus, the athlete should have some form of knob for the current period’s intensity, that can be quantified. E.g., number of sessions, how intense they are, etc., and the programs have clear guardrails based on these.
For example, if normally 3-4 sets of 5 reps at X%, and the athlete is in an intense sports period, with say, 5 intense sessions per week… then it should change to 1-2 sets of 5 reps at X%. So that it accommodates for recovery better, i.e., we change to a minimum effective dose without negative spillover. 

Here are some suggestions: 
The core problem
Fixed strength prescriptions assume a stable weekly stress. Combat sports weeks aren’t stable. If you keep strength volume constant while sport load spikes, you get:
accumulated fatigue → worse technical sessions


“missed reps” / grinding → negative adaptations


DOMS + soreness → even more spillover


So the app needs a recovery budget concept and a sport-load knob that automatically “steals” volume from strength when sport load rises.

MVP solution (simple, robust): Sport Load Level → Volume Multiplier
1) Athlete sets weekly “Sport Load Level”
Make it dead simple in UI:
1 = Low (off-season / light week)


2 = Normal


3 = High


4 = Peak (camp spike / competition week)


(Optionally you can let them set it per week, or the app suggests it based on logged sessions.)

| Athlete selects (1–4) | Label | How it feels | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Technical / Easy** | • Could do it again tomorrow with no problem. <br> • No “survival” moments. <br> Breathing stays under control. <br> • Next day: basically normal | **Boxing:** footwork + light bag + light pads, hard finisher. <br> **Wrestling/BJJ:** drilling + flow rolling, no hard live rounds. <br> **MMA:** technical work, low pace, lots of instruction breaks. |
| **2** | **Moderate / Productive** | • You worked and sweated, but you’re not wrecked. <br> You could do it tomorrow, but you’d notice accumulated fatigue if you repeated it. | **Boxing:** pads + bag at a steady pace, a few sharper bursts. <br> **Wrestling:** drilling + a few controlled live goes. <br> **BJJ:** normal rolls, but not against your hardest partners the whole time. |
| **3** | **Hard** | • You’re pushing. <br> Performance drops a bit near the end. <br> • Needs real recovery (sleep matters). <br> Next day: you feel it (heavy legs, slower snap), but you can still train. | **Boxing:** hard pads + hard conditioning finisher or tough sparring rounds (not all-out). <br> **Wrestling:** multiple hard live rounds / tough goes. <br> **BJJ:** many tough rolls where you’re fighting to not get smashed. |
| **4** | **Very hard / Fight-like** | • “Survival mode” at points. <br> Near-max effort. <br> Next day: clearly impacted (CNS fatigue / soreness / low pop). <br> Includes competition or true fight-sim. | **Boxing:** hard sparring with consequences / fight-simulation day. <br> **Wrestling:** all-out live rounds / test day. <br> **MMA:** brutal rounds + clinch grind + conditioning (or competition). |

2) App maps that level to multipliers (guardrails)
Rule: reduce volume first, not intensity.
Because intensity maintains strength/power better with minimal fatigue, while volume is what crushes recovery.
Example multipliers:
Low (1): strength volume × 1.00


Normal (2): × 0.85–1.00


High (3): × 0.60–0.75


Peak (4): × 0.40–0.55


3) What gets reduced (priority order)
Accessory sets (cut hardest)


Main lift back-off sets


Main lift intensity (only if needed)


4) Minimum Effective Dose guardrails
Even in Peak weeks, keep:
1–2 “hard sets” per main lift pattern per week (squat/hinge/press/pull)


Intensity exposure stays (e.g., keep the % the same, just fewer sets), or use a “top set @RPE cap” approach.


Concrete example (your exact case)
Normal prescription: 4×5 @ X%
 High sport week (5 intense sport sessions): apply High multiplier (~0.6)
4×5 → 2×5 @ X%
 Or if it’s really brutal: 1–2×5 @ X%
 That’s your “minimum effective dose without spillover.”
