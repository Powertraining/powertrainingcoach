# Striking sports
If no competition, general athleticism. If competition is known, peak for speed.
For boxing, kickboxing, and Muay Thai, the app must distinguish between Off-Camp and In-Camp.
Off-Camp = no confirmed competition inside the peak window. In this case, the program should prioritize general athletic development: build strength first, then power, while keeping some speed work present but not forcing a speed peak.
In-Camp = a confirmed competition exists inside the peak window. In this case, the program should back-plan from the event date and shift toward a speed-dominant peak in the final phase, while still maintaining enough strength and power to support speed.
For striking sports, the app should not chase maximal speed year-round. It should build the engine in Off-Camp, then express it as speed in Camp. This matches the existing rule that early phases emphasize traditional strength development, while late phases and peaking prioritize speed-strength, ballistic work, and more plyometric emphasis.
“Peak for pure speed” means peak in emphasis, not exclusive training.
In striking fight camp, the final phase should be speed-dominant, but the app must still preserve minimal effective doses of strength and power so the athlete does not lose force qualities while approaching competition. Volume should be reduced before intensity, and taper should reduce volume roughly 30–60% while keeping intensity and intent high
For striking sports, if no confirmed competition is within the peak window, program for general strength/power/speed with a build-focused emphasis and no forced speed peak. If a confirmed competition falls within the peak window, back-plan the program so the final phase peaks for speed, using strength and power development in earlier phases and a reduced-volume taper in the final 7–10 days.

## Periodization logic for Striking: 
For striking sports, the athlete needs enough maximal strength to create a high force ceiling, but fights are not won by displaying slow maximal force. They are won by producing usable force fast, repeatedly, while relaxed, coordinated, and fresh. So the closer the athlete gets to competition, the more the program should shift from “building force” to “expressing force quickly.”
1. Max strength matters, but it is not the final goal
Maximal strength is the base. A stronger athlete usually has more potential to produce power because they can apply more force into the floor, hips, trunk, and upper body. But in boxing, kickboxing, and Muay Thai, the decisive quality is not a slow grind. It is how quickly the athlete can produce force in a punch, kick, step, slip, counter, or combination.
So the logic is:
Off-camp: raise the ceiling.
 In-camp: convert the ceiling into speed and power.
 Peaking: express speed with minimal fatigue.
That is why heavy squats, deadlifts, presses, and pulls are more important farther away from a fight, while jumps, throws, fast lifts, reactive plyos, and striking-specific power become more important closer to the fight.
2. Speed is highly perishable and highly specific
A striker can be strong but still look slow if they are not regularly practicing high-velocity outputs. Speed depends on rate of force development, timing, coordination, elasticity, and relaxation. The punching-speed document makes this point clearly: unnecessary tension restricts speed and wastes energy, while efficient striking depends on relaxing non-essential muscles and coordinating the kinetic chain.
This is why late-camp training should not be dominated by grinding strength work. Heavy slow lifting creates fatigue and can interfere with sharpness. The athlete needs to feel snappy, loose, and reactive.
So in the final phase, the question becomes:
Does this exercise make the athlete faster and sharper, or just more tired?
If it makes them tired without improving speed expression, it should be reduced or removed.
3. Off-camp is for building qualities that take longer to develop
Strength, muscle/tendon capacity, structural balance, aerobic base, and general robustness take time. They are best built when there is no immediate fight pressure.
That is why off-camp should include more traditional strength development. There is less concern about carrying fatigue into sparring, weigh-ins, travel, or fight-week sharpness. The athlete can tolerate more volume and slower strength work because the goal is long-term development.
Off-camp is where we can say:
“Let’s make the athlete stronger, more durable, and more athletic overall.”
But even in off-camp, speed should not disappear completely. A striker should always touch speed and power in small doses so they do not become strong but sluggish.
4. In-camp is for conversion
Once a fight is scheduled, the S&C program becomes a support system for performance, not a separate strength project.
Early camp can still include heavier work because there is enough time to recover and adapt. But as the fight approaches, the strength work should shift toward maintenance. The power work should become more specific.
That creates a progression like:
General strength → maximal strength → strength-speed → speed-strength → fight-speed expression
This matches the broader power-development model from the MMA speed and power material: first develop maximal force, then high-velocity strength, then convert that athleticism into sport-specific velocity.
For striking, that means general jumps and med-ball throws early, then more lateral, rotational, elastic, and strike-specific exercises later.
5. Peaking is about freshness, not fitness-building
A common mistake is trying to “get stronger” or “get fitter” in the final 2–3 weeks. That is usually too late. The athlete should already be prepared. The goal now is to remove fatigue while preserving intensity and speed.
During peaking, the athlete still needs intensity, but not volume.
So instead of:
Heavy squat 5×5
Use:
Speed/Jump Squats, Ball Throws, Plyos, etc… Strength is included too, but not as the primary goal. Just for maintenance… and low volume. Majority of juice goes to power and speed.
6. Lower load does not mean lower effort
This is a key coaching point.
When we reduce load from 85–90% to 50–70%, the athlete should not treat the work as easy. The bar, body, or med ball should move violently fast. The source programs emphasize that power training requires maximal concentric intent, and that deliberately slow concentrics are a mistake when the goal is power.
So the late-camp message is:
The weight is lighter so you can move faster, not so you can relax mentally.
For example, a pause speed squat at 60–70% is not “light strength work.” It is a speed-strength tool. The pause removes the bounce, then the athlete has to explode out of the bottom with maximum intent.
7. Strikers need more elastic and reactive work late
Punching, kicking, slipping, stepping, and countering are not slow muscular actions. They are elastic, rhythmic, and reactive. That is why late-phase striking programs should include more plyometrics and ballistic work.
For boxing:
skater jumps, rotational throws, med-ball punch throws, reactive hops, bag speed bursts
For Muay Thai/kickboxing:
scissor jumps, split-squat jumps, single-leg bounds, rotational throws, explosive kick bursts
The exercise selection should move from general to specific:
General athleticism first. Fight-speed expression later.
This also reduces risk. You do not want to throw highly reactive or unfamiliar plyos at an athlete right before a fight. So take their experience into consideration. That is the caveat.
8. Fatigue management is more important in-camp
Striking camp already includes pads, bag work, sparring, footwork, clinch, drilling, roadwork, and often weight management. S&C has to fit into that, not compete with it.
That is why the athlete’s combat-sport load should control lifting volume. Rulebook already has a useful structure: moderate combat load can handle around 3–4 work sets per main lift, high load around 2 sets, and very high load only 1–2 sets.
This matters because a striker may not need more training. They may need better-timed training.
A tired athlete can still lift. But a tired athlete usually cannot express clean speed, timing, and relaxation.
9. The closer the fight, the more specific the output
Early in the process, a broad exercise like a squat, trap-bar deadlift, bench press, or pull-up is useful because it builds the body.
Late in the process, the program should ask:
“How does this help the athlete hit, kick, move, or react faster?”
That does not mean everything must mimic the sport exactly. It means the qualities should transfer: fast hip rotation, elastic footwork, rapid force into the floor, trunk stiffness/relaxation timing, and explosive upper-body projection.
The goal is not to turn the weight room into shadowboxing. The goal is to build qualities that show up in striking.
